# 1. Introduction

Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) and hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) convert wet biomass and residues into bio-oil (biocrude), a carbon-rich solid (hydrochar or char), an aqueous phase and gas without prior drying [@Toor2011; @Elliott2015; @Funke2010; @Libra2011]. Because product yields depend on feedstock composition, temperature, residence time, solid loading, catalysts and co-solvents, a large experimental literature has varied these factors one or a few at a time, and reviews have summarised the resulting trends qualitatively [@Akhtar2011; @Peterson2008; @Gollakota2018; @Dimitriadis2017]. More recently, machine-learning (ML) models trained on hundreds of literature-derived runs have been used to rank the importance of these factors and to optimise conditions [@Zhang2021BT; @Leng2022BT; @Katongtung2024; @Gungor2025; @Tian2025; @Djandja2027].

Both approaches face the same obstacle: results from different laboratories are not directly comparable. Each study uses its own feedstock batch, reactor, heating profile, product work-up and definition of a "yield"; bio-oil yield in particular depends on the recovery solvent [@Valdez2011]. Even analyses of identical liquefaction oils vary between laboratories [@IEARR2020]. When experiments from many studies are pooled, differences between laboratories are therefore mixed with the effects of the process variables. In an earlier analysis of the same database, study-level offsets made up {{solid_share}}–{{biooil_share}} of the error of literature-trained ML models for a study they had not seen (Supplementary Section S6; see also @Elfetni2026BT). How large the effects of the main process variables are once laboratory differences are removed, and how consistent they are from one laboratory to the next, has not been quantified.

Meta-analysis answers such questions in other fields of bioresource research, for example for microaeration in anaerobic digestion [@Microaeration2026], microplastics in anaerobic digestion [@Jiang2026MPAD] and carbon and nitrogen losses during composting [@Composting2026]. A systematic search of PubMed and Crossref found no meta-analysis of HTL or HTC process effects (Supplementary Section S1). The HTL/HTC literature offers an unusual opportunity: most studies vary one factor while holding the others constant. Laboratories differ far more in their absolute yields than in how their yields respond to such changes — in the ten longest temperature series of the database, bio-oil yields at 300 °C ranged from {{d24_lo}} to {{d24_hi}} wt%, whereas yields within each series changed much less with temperature (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig24_within_study_design}}). The effect of a factor can therefore be estimated *within* each study, where laboratory-specific offsets cancel, and then synthesised across studies with random-effects models that quantify the remaining between-laboratory heterogeneity [@DerSimonian1986; @Higgins2002; @Riley2011].

This study applies this design to the largest public curated hydrothermal database (3,693 experiments from 188 studies) [@Elfetni2026data]. Five questions were addressed under a protocol registered before any effect was computed: (1) How large are the within-study effects of temperature, residence time, solid loading, catalysts and reaction media on bio-oil and solid yields, and on the aqueous and gas phases, bio-oil heating value, energy recovery and nitrogen content? (2) How consistent are these effects between laboratories, and how likely is a given change to help in a new laboratory? (3) How large is the laboratory effect compared with the process effects? (4) Do pooled ML models and their importance measures, as commonly reported, recover the within-study effects? (5) Do the effects replicate in 38 studies that were held out from the start?

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Data source and study selection

The data were the Version 1.0.0 release of the curated hydrothermal treatment/liquefaction database [@Elfetni2026data] (CC BY 4.0; 3,693 runs from 188 source publications, 1982–2026; file checksum verified at every load). Each run records the source study, feedstock composition (ultimate, proximate and lignocellulosic analysis), temperature, residence time, solid loading, heating rate, catalyst and loading, reaction medium, atmosphere, process subtype (HTL, solvothermal HTL, HTC), yield basis, product separation procedure, product yields and product properties. Before any model was fitted in our earlier work on this database, 38 source studies (20%, seed 20261110) were drawn at random and locked away. They had been used in earlier evaluations of machine-learning transfer (Supplementary Section S6) but in no meta-analytic computation; here they were used once, for the replication test (Section 2.6). The remaining 150 studies formed the development set. Outcomes were bio-oil and solid yields (primary; wt%), and aqueous-phase and gas yields, bio-oil higher heating value (HHV; measured values only), energy recovery to bio-oil (yield × bio-oil HHV / feed HHV, %) and bio-oil nitrogen content (secondary). Yields reported on a carbon basis and values outside physical ranges were excluded. The database targets lignocellulosic and lignin-rich biomass: {{ma_lig_share}} of the {{ma_n_dev_studies}} development studies processed agricultural residues, wood, technical lignins or mixtures of these, and the rest manures, sludges, polymers or other feedstocks; {{ma_htl_runs}} development runs were HTL or solvothermal HTL and {{ma_htc_runs}} HTC. The reproducible literature search used to establish novelty and the relation of this work to earlier analyses of the same database [@Elfetni2026BT; @Elfetni2026SSRN] are described in Supplementary Section S1.

## 2.2. Within-study contrasts

Runs of the same study with identical values of every recorded condition except the factor of interest — feedstock identity and composition, temperature, residence time, solid loading, heating rate, catalyst class and loading, reaction medium, atmosphere and process subtype — form a *cell*. Five factors were analysed ({{TREF:table_v4_meta_primary}}). For temperature, residence time and solid loading, the effect is the slope between adjacent levels within a cell: change in the outcome per +10 °C (assigned to the band of the pair midpoint: < 250, 250–300, 300–350, ≥ 350 °C), per doubling of residence time and per +10 percentage points of solid loading. For catalysts, the effect is the mean outcome of runs with a given catalyst class (alkali carbonate, alkali hydroxide, acid, metal or supported metal, other) minus the mean outcome of uncatalysed runs in the same cell; for reaction media, the mean outcome with a co-solvent class (water–alcohol, alcohol, water with hydrogen donor, other organic) minus that in water. Feedstock effects were estimated by regressing the outcome on feedstock lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, ash and nitrogen contents across runs of the same cell that differ only in feedstock, with cell fixed effects and standard errors clustered by study. Because every contrast compares runs of the same study, laboratory-specific offsets — reactor, work-up, yield definition, analyst — cancel.

## 2.3. Synthesis

For each outcome, factor and level, the study-level effect was the mean of the study's contrasts, with sampling variance $s_w^2/m_j$, where $m_j$ is the number of contrasts of study $j$ and $s_w^2$ the pooled within-study variance of contrasts (experimental replicates are rarely reported, so within-study scatter of contrasts is the available measure of precision). Study effects were pooled with a random-effects model: the between-study variance $\tau^2$ was estimated by restricted maximum likelihood [@Viechtbauer2005; @Veroniki2016], confidence intervals used the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman correction [@Hartung2001; @Sidik2002], and 95% prediction intervals (PI), which describe the range of effects expected in a new study, were computed with $k-2$ degrees of freedom [@Riley2011; @IntHout2016]. Heterogeneity was summarised by $\tau$ and $I^2$ [@Higgins2002]. The probability that a change increases the outcome in a new laboratory, P(increase), was computed from the predictive distribution $N(\hat\mu, \hat\tau^2 + \mathrm{SE}^2)$. Results were reported when at least five studies contributed. Sensitivity analyses were the Paule–Mandel $\tau^2$ estimator, a one-contrast-per-study (median) estimator, leave-one-study-out ranges and an Egger-type test for small-study effects [@Egger1997] (≥ 10 studies). Pre-specified moderators of catalyst effects (cell temperature < 300 vs ≥ 300 °C, process subtype, feedstock lignin content above vs below the median) were tested with z-tests and Holm correction within outcome × factor.

## 2.4. Laboratory effect

The laboratory effect was quantified in two ways: (i) as the standard deviation $\hat\tau$ of study-level offsets of out-of-study residuals of a gradient-boosting model that uses feedstock composition and all recorded conditions (method of moments; Supplementary Section S6), and (ii) as the random-intercept standard deviation of a linear mixed model with the same inputs (piecewise-linear temperature, log residence time, loading, heating rate, catalyst and medium classes, feedstock composition, process subtype; fitted by REML). Both estimate the between-study variation in yield that is not explained by the recorded feedstock and conditions, and both were compared with the meta-analytic process effects.

## 2.5. Pooled models and importance measures

To test whether the pooled analyses common in the literature recover the within-study effects, three models were fitted to all development runs of each primary outcome: a pooled XGBoost model [@Chen2016] with hyper-parameters tuned by study-grouped cross-validation, a pooled linear model, and a linear model with study fixed effects (within transformation). Model-implied effects were computed on the scale of the meta-analytic effects: for XGBoost as partial-dependence differences [@Friedman2001] averaged over all runs (temperature between band limits; residence time and loading between their quartiles; catalyst class at its median loading versus none; medium class versus water), for the linear models from their coefficients. Agreement with the meta-analytic means was summarised by sign agreement for effects whose CI excluded zero, Spearman correlation and the share of model-implied effects outside the meta-analytic CI. TreeSHAP importances [@Lundberg2017] of the XGBoost model were summed within factor groups.

## 2.6. Replication in held-out studies

The 38 locked studies were analysed once, after all development analyses had been fixed. Their within-study contrasts were computed with the same definitions and predicted by (a) the development meta-analytic mean effect, (b) the pooled XGBoost model trained on all development runs (difference of its predictions for the two sides of the contrast) and (c) no change. The pre-registered primary test compared per-study root-mean-square errors of these predictions (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm correction); the coverage of the 95% PIs for the locked studies' effects was also recorded.

## 2.7. Protocol, deviations and software

The original protocol, the locked study list and five addenda were committed to a version-controlled repository before the analyses they govern; this meta-analysis was specified in Addendum E, registered before any effect size was computed (only the number of available contrasts had been counted). Two deviations were made after the first development run and before the locked analysis and are reported in Supplementary Section S2: levels closer than 5 °C, 0.25 log~2~ units of time or 1 percentage point of loading were not used as contrasts, because floating-point noise had produced near-zero denominators; and energy recovery was computed from yield and HHVs because the database's energy-yield column turned out to be yield × HHV/100 (MJ per kg feed). The protocol was not deposited in a public registry. Analyses used Python 3.13 with NumPy, pandas, SciPy, statsmodels and XGBoost; the random-effects estimator was validated against the Paule–Mandel implementation of statsmodels and with unit tests. All numbers in this article are inserted programmatically from the result files.
