# 1. Introduction

Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) and hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) convert wet lignocellulosic and residual biomass into bio-oil (biocrude), a carbon-rich solid (hydrochar or char), an aqueous phase and gas, without prior drying [@Toor2011; @Elliott2015; @Funke2010; @Libra2011]. Product yields depend non-linearly on feedstock composition, temperature, residence time, solid loading, catalysts and co-solvents [@Gollakota2018], and experiments are costly. Machine-learning (ML) surrogates trained on data compiled from the literature have therefore become a common tool for predicting yields and product properties and for screening operating windows [@Zhang2021BT; @Leng2022BT; @Katongtung2024; @Gungor2025; @Tian2025; @Djandja2027]. In a systematic PubMed search of ML studies on hydrothermal conversion (2015–2026; {{lit_n_primary}} primary studies, {{lit_n_biortech}} of them in *Bioresource Technology*), the median of the best coefficient of determination reported in the abstracts was {{lit_median_r2}} (Supplementary Table S1).

Most of these surrogates are evaluated by randomly partitioning experimental runs into training and test sets. Literature-compiled data, however, are clustered: each source publication contributes a series of runs that share the feedstock batch, reactor, analytical work-up and the authors' definition of a "yield". When runs from the same study fall on both sides of a random split, the test set no longer represents a new study, and accuracy is overstated — a form of group leakage that has been documented across ML-based science [@Kapoor2023] and, recently, for biochar studies [@Jang2026]. For HTL specifically, @Elfetni2026BT curated a database of 3,693 hydrothermal experiments [@Elfetni2026data], built physically admissible four-phase surrogates, and showed that publication-aware validation exposes large inter-study heterogeneity that a few local calibration runs can partly remove. Prediction intervals have also been added to HTL surrogates [@Elfetni2026SSRN; @Djandja2027], but their coverage has been assessed on randomly held-out runs.

Three questions that matter for anyone applying a published surrogate to a new laboratory, feedstock or plant remain open. First, how much of the error on an unseen study is a study-level offset rather than run-to-run scatter, and can it be traced to recorded differences in how studies work up and report their products (extraction solvent, yield basis, fraction definitions, reactor scale)? Bio-oil yield is an operationally defined quantity: it depends on the solvent used to recover the oil and on the basis of the reported mass fraction [@Valdez2011; @Watson2019]. Second, do conformal prediction intervals [@Vovk2005; @Lei2018; @Angelopoulos2023], whose validity rests on exchangeability between calibration and test points, keep their nominal coverage when the test point comes from a new study? Exchangeability holds across studies, not across runs of a clustered data set [@Dunn2023; @Barber2023]. Third, when a laboratory has measured only a handful of runs, how should these runs be combined with a literature-trained model so that both the prediction and its interval remain trustworthy?

A fourth question follows from the structure of the problem. If most of the error for a new study is a study-wide offset, *differences* between runs of the same study — how much the yield changes when temperature or residence time is changed — might transfer even when absolute yields do not. Learners that are invariant to study offsets, such as models trained on within-study deviations or pairwise-difference regression [@Tynes2021], could then be useful for planning experiments, and multi-study learning has been proposed for exactly such replicability problems [@Patil2018]. Comparable limitations of literature-derived data have been reported for reaction-yield prediction in chemistry [@LitYield2024], but whether literature-trained models carry *trends* to new studies has, to our knowledge, not been tested for bioresource conversion.

This work audits what literature-trained models can deliver for a new laboratory, using the largest public curated hydrothermal database [@Elfetni2026data], a pre-registered analysis plan and 38 source studies locked away before any model was fitted. Five capabilities were tested, each against held-out studies: (i) predicting absolute yields; (ii) explaining study offsets from reported work-up and yield-basis metadata; (iii) predicting within-study trends, with absolute, within-study and pairwise-difference learners and with a model-free analysis of runs that differ only in temperature or residence time; (iv) choosing the best of a study's planned runs, compared with rules of thumb; and (v) providing honest uncertainty and exploiting a few local runs. For (v) the study assembles established statistical constructions — study-weighted conformal calibration for hierarchical data [@Dunn2023], empirical-Bayes shrinkage of a study offset [@Efron1975] and re-calibration over exchangeable calibration groups as in meta-learned and small-area conformal prediction [@Fisch2021; @Bersson2024] — into a workflow (STRATA). All pre-registered hypotheses are reported, including those that failed. The evidence is condensed into a capability matrix, a model-free trend atlas and a minimum validation checklist for literature-trained bioresource surrogates. Physical admissibility of multi-phase predictions, already addressed by @Elfetni2026BT, is not a contribution of this study.

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Data

The primary data were the Version 1.0.0 release of the curated hydrothermal treatment/liquefaction database [@Elfetni2026data] (CC BY 4.0; 3,693 runs from 188 source publications, 1982–2026; SHA-256 of the file verified at every load). Each run records the source DOI, feedstock ultimate, proximate and lignocellulosic composition, operating conditions, catalyst, reaction medium, the reported yield basis and separation procedure, and product yields. Two targets were modelled: bio-oil yield (HTL and solvothermal HTL runs) and solid-product yield (HTL, solvothermal HTL and HTC runs), both in wt%. Pre-specified exclusions were: aqueous-phase reforming runs; yields outside 0–100 wt%; yields reported on a carbon basis; missing feedstock C or O, temperature or residence time; and studies contributing a single run to a target (Table 1). An independent external data set — a 4TU.ResearchData hydrochar database [@TU4data] (CC BY 4.0) without study identifiers — was used only for external validation of solid yield (Section 2.6).

## 2.2. Locked study-level test set and leakage controls

Before any model was fitted, 20% of the source publications (38 studies, seed 20261110) were drawn at random and locked (`locked_test_studies.json`, committed to version control before the first model fit). They were evaluated once, after all modelling decisions had been frozen, with models trained on all development studies. The analysis protocol, research questions, metrics, statistical tests and seeds were registered in the repository before experiments were run; three deviations are documented in Supplementary Section S2. Leakage controls were: the unit of splitting is the source publication; DOI, title and publication year are never model inputs (year is used only to define the temporal split); all statistics-estimating preprocessing (median imputation with missing-value indicators, standardisation) is refitted inside each training fold; the external database was screened for runs that duplicate runs of the primary database ({{ext_dups}} potential duplicates removed); and the primary database was audited for identical runs reported in more than one source study (Section 2.6, E10).

## 2.3. Features

*Base features* describe chemistry and conditions: feedstock C, H, O, N and ash; lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose; temperature; log residence time; solid loading; heating rate; catalyst class (alkali carbonate, alkali hydroxide, acid, metal/supported metal, other) and catalyst-to-biomass ratio; reaction-medium class (water, alcohol, water–alcohol, water with hydrogen donor or co-reactant, ketone, other organic); gas pre-pressurisation; and process subtype. *Protocol/reporting descriptors* (P) encode how a study defined and recovered its products, parsed deterministically from the curated yield-basis and separation fields: dry-ash-free basis; basis including co-reactant or solvent mass; product defined as several combined fractions; aqueous or gas phase obtained by difference; extraction solvent (dichloromethane, acetone, ethyl acetate, diethyl ether, tetrahydrofuran, alcohol, hydrocarbon, other chlorinated, none named); separation route not stated; and log reactor volume. Parsing rules are in `src/data/load.py` and unit-tested.

## 2.4. Learners, tuning and evaluation schemes

Twelve learners were compared: mean predictor, ridge regression, *k*-nearest neighbours, support-vector regression, multilayer perceptron, random forest [@Breiman2001], extra trees [@Geurts2006], histogram gradient boosting [@Pedregosa2011], LightGBM [@Ke2017], XGBoost [@Chen2016], CatBoost [@Prokhorenkova2018] and GPBoost, which adds a study-level random intercept to tree boosting [@Sigrist2022]. A tabular foundation model [@Hollmann2025] was pre-specified but could not be run because its weights require a personal licence acceptance (Supplementary Section S2). Every learner received the same tuning budget — 20 random hyper-parameter configurations scored by study-grouped five-fold cross-validation (CV) on the development studies — separately for each feature set; the selected configuration was then frozen. Each learner was evaluated by (a) random-row five-fold CV, the prevailing practice, and (b) study-grouped ten-fold CV repeated with three pre-specified seeds, in which every development study is predicted by a model that has never seen any of its runs [@Roberts2017]. Metrics were RMSE, MAE and R² over all runs and the RMSE of each study.

## 2.5. STRATA

STRATA wraps any point predictor $f$. Let $r_{ij} = y_{ij} - f(\mathbf{x}_{ij})$ be the residual of run $i$ of study $j$ obtained *out of study*, i.e. from a model that did not see study $j$ (inner study-grouped five-fold CV on the training studies).

*Heterogeneity decomposition.* Residuals are modelled as $r_{ij} = u_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$ with study offset $u_j$ (variance $\tau^2$) and within-study error $\varepsilon_{ij}$ (variance $\sigma_w^2$). Both are estimated by the unbalanced one-way ANOVA method of moments [@DerSimonian1986],
$$\hat\sigma_w^2 = \mathrm{MS}_W, \qquad \hat\tau^2 = \max\!\left(0, \frac{\mathrm{MS}_B - \mathrm{MS}_W}{n_0}\right), \qquad n_0 = \frac{N - \sum_j n_j^2 / N}{J - 1},$$
and the between-study share is $\hat\tau^2/(\hat\tau^2+\hat\sigma_w^2)$, analogous to $I^2$ in meta-analysis [@Higgins2002]; 95% CIs are obtained by resampling studies (2,000 cluster-bootstrap replicates [@Efron1979]). To test whether P explains study offsets, the mean out-of-study residual of each development study (base-feature model) was regressed on the study means of P by ridge regression with leave-one-study-out prediction; significance was assessed by permuting offsets across studies (1,000 permutations).

*Study-calibrated conformal intervals (SC).* For a new run the interval is $f(\mathbf{x}) \pm \hat q$, where $\hat q$ is the weighted $(1-\alpha)$ empirical quantile of $|r_{ij}|$ in which every calibration study receives the same total weight (run weight $1/n_j$), with the usual finite-sample correction. This is the CDF-pooling construction for two-layer hierarchical data of @Dunn2023, under which the target of validity is a run from a new study that is exchangeable with the calibration studies; its guarantee is approximate rather than exact in finite samples, and it holds marginally over studies, not for every individual study. As in cross-validation-based conformal methods, the calibration residuals come from inner models fitted on subsets of the training studies, whereas test predictions come from the model fitted on all training studies — a standard, slightly conservative approximation. The comparator, *row-calibrated* intervals (RC), uses the unweighted quantile of residuals from random-row CV — the construction implied when conformal calibration is performed on a random split.

*k-shot empirical-Bayes adaptation (KS).* When $k$ runs of the new study have been measured, the predictions for its remaining runs are shifted by
$$\hat\delta_k = \frac{k}{k + \hat\lambda}\,\bar r_{S}, \qquad \hat\lambda = \hat\sigma_w^2 / \hat\tau^2,$$
where $\bar r_S$ is the mean residual of the $k$ support runs. This is the posterior mean of $u_j$ under a normal random-intercept model, a James–Stein-type shrinkage [@Efron1975] that tends to the raw mean offset as $k$ grows and to zero when studies are homogeneous. The interval half-width $\hat q_k$ is re-calibrated by applying exactly this procedure to the calibration studies (20 random support sets per study) and taking the study-weighted quantile of the adjusted residuals of their query runs. The ablation compares no offset ($k=0$), the raw mean offset and the shrunk offset.

## 2.6. Experiments

E1/E2 (accuracy and baselines) applied the two evaluation schemes to all learners and both feature sets. E3 (STRATA) used the study-grouped outer folds: for each fold, inner random-row and inner study-grouped CV on the training studies produced RC and SC calibration residuals and $\hat\lambda$; the outer test studies were then predicted, and for studies with at least 11 runs the *k*-shot procedure was evaluated for $k \in \{1,2,3,5,10\}$ with 20 random support sets per study (identical studies for all $k$). The base learner for STRATA was fixed by a pre-specified rule as the native-missing-value learner with the lowest study-grouped CV RMSE on bio-oil yield ({{strata_learner_label}}). Robustness (E4) was assessed by adding Gaussian noise (5–20% of the training standard deviation) to continuous test features and by training on 25–100% of the training studies. Generalisation (E5) was assessed on the locked test studies, temporally (studies published up to 2018 → studies published after 2018), by leaving out one feedstock family at a time (families with ≥ 3 studies), and externally on the 4TU hydrochar database (solid yield; runs sharing an identical feedstock composition treated as one pseudo-study). E6 measured single-thread fit and prediction time, peak resident memory and serialized model size. E7 varied α (0.05, 0.10, 0.20), the $\tau^2$ estimator (method of moments vs. restricted maximum likelihood), the number of outer folds (5 vs. 10) and tuning (tuned vs. library defaults). E8 analysed out-of-study errors by feedstock family, process, extraction solvent, temperature, catalyst use and yield basis. Three further analyses were added during internal review, after the main results were known, and are reported as such: E9 repeated the STRATA evaluation with six other base learners, including the training mean as a 'no-ML' reference (development folds only); E10 audited runs that occur with identical feedstock composition, conditions and yield in more than one source study, and repeated the study-grouped evaluation without them; E11 measured the wall-clock overhead of STRATA.

## 2.7. Trend, ranking and anchoring analyses (pre-registered addenda)

These analyses were registered as protocol addenda after the main analyses had been completed and before they were run (Supplementary Section S2): Addendum A (E12) before any trend analysis, Addendum A2 before analysing two product-quality targets, and Addendum C before any locked-study evaluation of E12 or E14. Addendum B (E14) was registered after the development results of E12 had been seen and is exploratory; its main finding was then tested on the locked studies under Addendum C.

*Within-study contrasts (E12).* For two runs *a* and *b* of the same study, the contrast is $\Delta_{ab} = y_a - y_b$; a study offset cancels in $\Delta_{ab}$. Three learners predicted contrasts for every pair of runs within each unseen study: the absolute model A, by differencing, $\hat\Delta_{ab} = f(\mathbf{x}_a) - f(\mathbf{x}_b)$; a within-study model W, trained on study-centred targets $y - \bar y_{\text{study}}$ with features $[\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{study}}]$ (the study means of the inputs are known for a new study before any run is measured); and a pairwise-difference model PD [@Tynes2021], trained on pairs of runs of the same training study with inputs $[\mathbf{x}_a, \mathbf{x}_b, \mathbf{x}_a - \mathbf{x}_b]$ and target $\Delta_{ab}$ (at most 30 random partners per run), antisymmetrised as $\hat\Delta_{ab} = \tfrac{1}{2}[h(a,b) - h(b,a)]$. All three used the E1 hyper-parameters of XGBoost, extra trees and LightGBM. Contrast accuracy was summarised by $R^2_\Delta = 1 - \sum(\hat\Delta - \Delta)^2 / \sum \Delta^2$, i.e. relative to the 'no-change' predictor. Hypotheses: contrasts are predicted better than levels (T1); W and PD predict contrasts better than A (T2, one-sided Wilcoxon on per-study contrast RMSE, Holm); anchoring on *k* = 1–3 measured runs, $\hat y_i = \tfrac{1}{k}\sum_a (y_a + \hat\Delta_{ia})$, beats STRATA's shrunk offset (T3); study-calibrated conformal intervals for contrasts are valid and narrower than for levels (T4); and models rank the runs of a study usefully (T5). The same analysis was repeated for bio-oil higher heating value (HHV; values computed by correlation excluded; {{n_hhv_biooil_dev}} development runs from {{s_hhv_biooil_dev}} studies, {{n_hhv_biooil_test}} locked runs) and bio-oil carbon content ({{n_c_biooil_dev}} runs, {{s_c_biooil_dev}} studies; {{n_c_biooil_test}} locked runs) (Addendum A2; XGBoost tuned with the E1 budget).

*Model-free trends (E13).* Within each development study, pairs of runs that differ only in temperature (all other base features and the feedstock identical) or only in residence time were identified. Their observed slopes (change in yield per +10 °C, or per doubling of residence time) were summarised per study and temperature band; medians across studies were bootstrapped over studies, and the fraction of studies whose slope has the same sign as the median was recorded.

*Contrast types and choosing conditions (E14, exploratory; confirmed under Addendum C).* Contrast accuracy was split into temperature-only, residence-time-only and other pairs. For each unseen study with ≥ 5 runs, the run with the highest predicted yield (A: highest $f$; W: highest $g$; PD: highest mean predicted contrast against the other runs) was compared with a rule of thumb — for bio-oil, the run with temperature closest to 325 °C, the centre of the optimum band in E13; for solids, the lowest-temperature run — and with random choice, using the regret $\max_i y_i - y_{\text{chosen}}$. The bio-oil comparison was then tested once, one-sided, on the locked studies (Holm over the three learners).

## 2.8. Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis for all inference was the source study. Learners and STRATA variants were compared through per-study RMSE (averaged over seeds) with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [@Wilcoxon1945]; families of comparisons were corrected with Holm's step-down procedure as described by @Demsar2006. Effect sizes are the median paired difference with a 95% bootstrap CI over studies and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation. One-sided tests were used only for directional hypotheses registered in advance (P reduces error; SC coverage exceeds RC coverage). Coverage was summarised both over runs and with equal study weights, with 95% cluster-bootstrap CIs; on development folds, point estimates average the three outer seeds and CIs were computed for the first seed. Statistical significance is claimed only for Holm-adjusted *p* < 0.05.

## 2.9. Software and reproducibility

All analyses ran on a 4-core CPU without GPU using Python 3.13, scikit-learn, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, GPBoost, statsmodels and SciPy (exact versions in `requirements.txt`). A single command (`python run_all.py --mode full`) regenerates every number, table and figure from the vendored data; a quick mode, run from a fresh clone of the repository, verified the whole pipeline in {{quick_minutes}} minutes on the same machine, and the full analysis stages reproduced every value in this article exactly. All values reported in this article are inserted programmatically from the generated result files.
