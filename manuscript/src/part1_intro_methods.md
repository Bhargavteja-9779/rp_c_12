# 1. Introduction

Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) and hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) convert wet lignocellulosic and residual biomass into bio-oil (biocrude), a carbon-rich solid (hydrochar or char), an aqueous phase and gas, without prior drying [@Toor2011; @Elliott2015; @Funke2010; @Libra2011]. Product yields depend non-linearly on feedstock composition, temperature, residence time, solid loading, catalysts and co-solvents [@Gollakota2018], and each experiment is costly. Machine-learning (ML) models trained on runs compiled from the literature have therefore become a common tool for predicting yields and for screening operating windows [@Zhang2021BT; @Leng2022BT; @Katongtung2024; @Gungor2025; @Tian2025; @Djandja2027]. A reproducible search of PubMed and Crossref (run {{lit3_date}}; full strings, eligibility rules and record flow in {{TREF:tableS_v3_prisma}}) identified {{lit3_included}} primary ML studies of hydrothermal conversion published since 2015, {{lit3_bt}} of them in *Bioresource Technology*. Of the {{lit3_with_abs}} with an abstract, {{lit3_grouped}} mentioned grouped or external validation and {{lit3_uq}} any form of uncertainty quantification; in a PubMed subset reporting R², the median best R² was {{lit_median_r2}} ({{lit_n_r2}} studies; {{TREF:tableS1_literature}}).

Such models are built to be used by a laboratory that was not part of the training data — a group that has a new feedstock and reactor and must decide which conditions to run first. For this *new-laboratory deployment problem* four things matter: a prediction of the yields before any experiment, an honest statement of its uncertainty, guidance on how many and which first runs to perform, and a way to tell whether the literature model or the laboratory's own first runs should be trusted. Random splits of literature data answer none of these questions. Literature-compiled data are clustered: each source publication contributes a series of runs that share the feedstock batch, reactor, analytical work-up and the authors' definition of a "yield". When runs of the same study fall on both sides of a random split, the test set no longer represents a new laboratory, and accuracy is overstated — a form of group leakage documented across ML-based science [@Kapoor2023] and for biochar studies [@Jang2026]. Bio-oil yield is, moreover, operationally defined by the recovery solvent and the reporting basis [@Valdez2011; @Watson2019], so study-specific offsets are expected.

Two recent studies used the same public database [@Elfetni2026data] and partly address these issues. @Elfetni2026BT built physically admissible four-phase HTL surrogates on a closure-valid subset of 680 experiments, reported that publication-aware validation exposes inter-study heterogeneity, showed that five local records reduced the median error of the publications eligible for local calibration, and reported split-conformal coverage on a random test partition. A preprint by the same group [@Elfetni2026SSRN] modelled energy and carbon routing with ensemble and conformal uncertainty for screening and experimental prioritisation. Bootstrap and Monte Carlo uncertainty has also been propagated through HTL yield models [@Djandja2027]. What these studies leave open, and what a new laboratory needs to know, is (i) whether prediction intervals keep their nominal coverage for studies that were never seen, and how wide honest intervals are; (ii) whether combining a few local runs with the literature model is better than simply using the local runs, at each number of runs, when the runs are chosen as a laboratory would choose them and intervals are re-calibrated accordingly; (iii) whether a laboratory can tell in advance that the literature model will mislead it; and (iv) whether anything beyond a level shift — effects of conditions, or the ranking of candidate runs — transfers between laboratories. {{TREF:table_v3_novelty}} sets out how the present work relates to the two studies; none of the present authors is an author of either study, and the overlap is the openly licensed database.

{{TABLE:table_v3_novelty}}

This work addresses (i)–(iv) with a framework called STRATA-Lab (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig16_strata_lab_workflow}}). Its statistical core, STRATA, assembles established constructions — study-weighted conformal calibration for hierarchical data [@Dunn2023], empirical-Bayes shrinkage of a study offset [@Efron1975] and re-calibration over exchangeable calibration groups as in meta-learned and small-area conformal prediction [@Fisch2021; @Bersson2024] — around any point predictor. STRATA-Lab adds the deployment steps: a literature prediction with a study-calibrated interval, an applicability-domain score, first runs placed by a simple screening design, a comparison of literature-anchored and local-only estimates, and the number of runs needed for a target interval width. The framework was evaluated by retrospective simulated deployment on published data — on unseen development studies, on 38 studies locked away before any model was fitted, and on an independent hydrochar database — and illustrated with two laboratory case studies selected by a pre-specified rule. The scope is limited to bio-oil and solid yields; aqueous and gas phases and mass closure, addressed by @Elfetni2026BT, are not modelled, and no new experiments were performed. All analyses specified in advance are reported, including those whose hypotheses failed.

# 2. Materials and methods

## 2.1. Data

The primary data were the Version 1.0.0 release of the curated hydrothermal treatment/liquefaction database [@Elfetni2026data] (CC BY 4.0; 3,693 runs from 188 source publications, 1982–2026; SHA-256 of the file verified at every load). Each run records the source DOI, feedstock ultimate, proximate and lignocellulosic composition, operating conditions, catalyst, reaction medium, the reported yield basis and separation procedure, and product yields. Two targets were modelled: bio-oil yield (HTL and solvothermal HTL runs) and solid-product yield (HTL, solvothermal HTL and HTC runs), both in wt%. Pre-specified exclusions were: aqueous-phase reforming runs; yields outside 0–100 wt%; yields reported on a carbon basis; missing feedstock C or O, temperature or residence time; and studies contributing a single run to a target ({{TREF:table1_data}}). An independent external data set — a 4TU.ResearchData hydrochar database [@TU4data] (CC BY 4.0) without study identifiers — was used only for external validation of solid yield (Sections 2.6–2.7).

## 2.2. Locked study-level test set, pre-specification and leakage controls

Before any model was fitted, 20% of the source publications (38 studies, seed 20261110) were drawn at random and locked. They were evaluated once per registered analysis, after all modelling decisions for that analysis had been frozen, with models trained on all development studies. The analysis protocol (research questions, metrics, statistical tests, seeds and the locked list) and five later addenda were registered as timestamped commits in a version-controlled repository hosted on GitHub before the analyses they govern were run; the protocol was not deposited in a public registry, so this is weaker than formal pre-registration, and the commit history is reported for verification ({{TREF:table_v2_timeline}}). Addendum D, which specifies the deployment analyses of Section 2.6, was registered after an external pre-submission review and before any of those analyses was run. Deviations are listed in Supplementary Section S2. Leakage controls were: the unit of splitting is the source publication; DOI, title and publication year are never model inputs; all statistics-estimating preprocessing (median imputation with missing-value indicators, standardisation) is refitted inside each training fold; the external database was screened for runs that duplicate runs of the primary database ({{ext_dups}} potential duplicates removed); and cross-study duplicate runs in the primary database were audited (Section 3.7).

## 2.3. Features

*Base features* describe chemistry and conditions: feedstock C, H, O, N and ash; lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose; temperature; log residence time; solid loading; heating rate; catalyst class (alkali carbonate, alkali hydroxide, acid, metal/supported metal, other) and catalyst-to-biomass ratio; reaction-medium class (water, alcohol, water–alcohol, water with hydrogen donor or co-reactant, ketone, other organic); gas pre-pressurisation; and process subtype. *Protocol/reporting descriptors* (P) encode how a study defined and recovered its products, parsed deterministically from the curated yield-basis and separation fields: dry-ash-free basis; basis including co-reactant or solvent mass; product defined as several combined fractions; aqueous or gas phase obtained by difference; extraction solvent (dichloromethane, acetone, ethyl acetate, diethyl ether, tetrahydrofuran, alcohol, hydrocarbon, other chlorinated, none named); separation route not stated; and log reactor volume. Parsing rules are in `src/data/load.py` and unit-tested.

## 2.4. Learners, tuning and evaluation schemes

Twelve learners were compared: mean predictor, ridge regression, *k*-nearest neighbours, support-vector regression, multilayer perceptron, random forest [@Breiman2001], extra trees [@Geurts2006], histogram gradient boosting [@Pedregosa2011], LightGBM [@Ke2017], XGBoost [@Chen2016], CatBoost [@Prokhorenkova2018] and GPBoost, which adds a study-level random intercept to tree boosting [@Sigrist2022]. A tabular foundation model [@Hollmann2025] was pre-specified but could not be run because its weights require a personal licence acceptance (Supplementary Section S2). Every learner received the same tuning budget — 20 random hyper-parameter configurations scored by study-grouped five-fold cross-validation (CV) on the development studies — separately for each feature set; the selected configuration was then frozen. Each learner was evaluated by (a) random-row five-fold CV, the prevailing practice, and (b) study-grouped ten-fold CV repeated with three pre-specified seeds, in which every development study is predicted by a model that has never seen any of its runs [@Roberts2017]. Metrics were RMSE, MAE and R² over all runs and the RMSE of each study.

## 2.5. STRATA

STRATA wraps any point predictor $f$. Let $r_{ij} = y_{ij} - f(\mathbf{x}_{ij})$ be the residual of run $i$ of study $j$ obtained *out of study*, i.e. from a model that did not see study $j$ (inner study-grouped five-fold CV on the training studies).

*Heterogeneity decomposition.* Residuals are modelled as $r_{ij} = u_j + \varepsilon_{ij}$ with study offset $u_j$ (variance $\tau^2$) and within-study error $\varepsilon_{ij}$ (variance $\sigma_w^2$). Both are estimated by the unbalanced one-way ANOVA method of moments [@DerSimonian1986],
$$\hat\sigma_w^2 = \mathrm{MS}_W, \qquad \hat\tau^2 = \max\!\left(0, \frac{\mathrm{MS}_B - \mathrm{MS}_W}{n_0}\right), \qquad n_0 = \frac{N - \sum_j n_j^2 / N}{J - 1},$$
and the between-study share is $\hat\tau^2/(\hat\tau^2+\hat\sigma_w^2)$, analogous to $I^2$ in meta-analysis [@Higgins2002]; 95% CIs are obtained by resampling studies (2,000 cluster-bootstrap replicates [@Efron1979]). To test whether P explains study offsets, the mean out-of-study residual of each development study (base-feature model) was regressed on the study means of P by ridge regression with leave-one-study-out prediction; significance was assessed by permuting offsets across studies (1,000 permutations).

*Study-calibrated conformal intervals (SC).* For a new run the interval is $f(\mathbf{x}) \pm \hat q$, where $\hat q$ is the weighted $(1-\alpha)$ empirical quantile of $|r_{ij}|$ in which every calibration study receives the same total weight (run weight $1/n_j$), with the usual finite-sample correction. This is the CDF-pooling construction for two-layer hierarchical data of @Dunn2023, under which the target of validity is a run from a new study that is exchangeable with the calibration studies; its guarantee is approximate rather than exact in finite samples, and it holds marginally over studies, not for every individual study. As in cross-validation-based conformal methods, the calibration residuals come from inner models fitted on subsets of the training studies, whereas test predictions come from the model fitted on all training studies — a standard, slightly conservative approximation. The comparator, *row-calibrated* intervals (RC), uses the unweighted quantile of residuals from random-row CV — the construction implied when conformal calibration is performed on a random split.

*k-shot empirical-Bayes adaptation (KS).* When $k$ runs of the new study have been measured, the predictions for its remaining runs are shifted by
$$\hat\delta_k = \frac{k}{k + \hat\lambda}\,\bar r_{S}, \qquad \hat\lambda = \hat\sigma_w^2 / \hat\tau^2,$$
where $\bar r_S$ is the mean residual of the $k$ support runs. This is the posterior mean of $u_j$ under a normal random-intercept model, a James–Stein-type shrinkage [@Efron1975] that tends to the raw mean offset as $k$ grows and to zero when studies are homogeneous. The interval half-width $\hat q_k$ is re-calibrated by applying exactly this procedure to the calibration studies (20 random support sets per study) and taking the study-weighted quantile of the adjusted residuals of their query runs. The ablation compares no offset ($k=0$), the raw mean offset and the shrunk offset.

## 2.6. STRATA-Lab: retrospective simulated deployment (Addendum D)

All deployment analyses are retrospective simulations on published runs: a study that was not used for training plays the role of a new laboratory, and its reported runs play the role of the experiments it would perform. The learner was the STRATA base learner with base and protocol features, and the nominal miscoverage was α = 0.10.

*Sequential local adaptation (E15).* For every unseen study with at least 11 runs, the runs were put in an order and the first *k* ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3, 5} were treated as measured (support runs). The runs from position 6 onward formed a fixed query set, identical for all *k*, so that support and query runs never overlap. Three orders were used: a *screening design* (main analysis) — first the run whose temperature is closest to the study's median temperature, then the lowest- and the highest-temperature runs, and further runs by maximin distance in standardised temperature and log residence time; the *reported order* of the runs in the database, which follows the source tables and is not chronological; and 20 *random* orders. Three strategies predicted the query runs: A, the literature model without adaptation; B, STRATA, the literature model shifted by the shrunk mean residual of the *k* support runs (Section 2.5); and C, local only, the mean of the *k* support runs. For each strategy, order and *k*, the 90% interval was calibrated by applying exactly the same procedure — same order rule, same *k*, same query definition — to the calibration studies (out-of-study predictions for A and B, observed yields for C) and taking the study-weighted quantile of the absolute query residuals. Metrics were per-study RMSE and MAE on the query runs, study-weighted coverage and mean interval width. The evaluation was run on unseen development studies (study-grouped ten-fold CV, three seeds), once on the locked studies, and on the external hydrochar database (solid yield; feedstock series as pseudo-studies; base features; model trained on all development studies). B was compared with A and with C at each *k* by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-study RMSE, with Holm correction within each setting × target × order.

*Decision support (E16).* (a) An applicability-domain (AD) score was computed for each study as the median, over its runs, of the mean Euclidean distance to the five nearest training runs in standardised base features. The threshold was fixed in advance as the 95th percentile of the scores of development studies in out-of-study folds; hypothesis D1 was that locked studies above the threshold have a larger error of strategy A (one-sided Mann–Whitney test). (b) The number of local runs needed for a target 90% half-width *h* ∈ {10, 15, 20} wt% was read from the development results of strategy B (screening-design order) and checked for coverage on the locked studies. (c) The rule "use C if a study is flagged, otherwise B" was compared with always using B on the locked studies at *k* = 3 (D2).

*Laboratory case studies (E17).* For each target, the locked study with at least 11 runs and the largest number of distinct temperatures (ties broken by the number of runs) was selected by rule before results were inspected. Its runs were measured one at a time in screening-design order, and after each run all remaining runs were predicted by A, B and C with intervals calibrated as in E15 (remaining-runs query definition). Reported are the error after each run, interval width and coverage, and the regret of choosing the remaining run with the highest predicted yield (best observed yield minus observed yield of the chosen run). Because B adds a constant offset, it ranks runs exactly as A does.

## 2.7. Further analyses

E1/E2 (accuracy and baselines) compared twelve learners under random-row and study-grouped CV with both feature sets. E3 (STRATA) produced the heterogeneity decomposition, the row- versus study-calibrated interval comparison and the earlier *k*-shot evaluation with random support sets ({{TREF:table5_kshot}}). Robustness (E4: input noise, fewer training studies), generalisation (E5: locked studies, studies published after 2018 predicted from earlier ones, leave-one-feedstock-family-out, external database), cost (E6, E11), sensitivity (E7: α, $\tau^2$ estimator, number of folds, tuning) and error analysis by subgroup (E8) followed the registered protocol. E9 (STRATA with six other base learners), E10 (cross-study duplicate audit) and E11 were added during internal review after the main results were known and are reported as such. Addenda A, A2, B and C tested whether within-study *changes* transfer better than absolute yields: within-study contrasts $\Delta_{ab} = y_a - y_b$, in which a study offset cancels, were predicted by the absolute model, a within-study-centred model and a pairwise-difference model [@Tynes2021]; model-free temperature slopes were computed from pairs of runs of the same study that differ only in temperature (E13, descriptive, not pre-registered); and the run predicted to give the highest yield was compared with rules of thumb (E14, exploratory, then tested once on locked studies). Details are given in Supplementary Section S7.

## 2.8. Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis for all inference was the source study. Learners and STRATA variants were compared through per-study RMSE (averaged over seeds) with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [@Wilcoxon1945]; families of comparisons were corrected with Holm's step-down procedure as described by @Demsar2006. Effect sizes are the median paired difference with a 95% bootstrap CI over studies and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation. One-sided tests were used only for directional hypotheses registered in advance (P reduces error; SC coverage exceeds RC coverage). Coverage was summarised both over runs and with equal study weights, with 95% cluster-bootstrap CIs; on development folds, point estimates average the three outer seeds and CIs were computed for the first seed. Statistical significance is claimed only for Holm-adjusted *p* < 0.05.

## 2.9. Software and availability of code

All analyses ran on a 4-core CPU without GPU using Python 3.13, scikit-learn, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, GPBoost, statsmodels and SciPy (exact versions recorded with the code). A single command regenerates every number, table and figure from the public data; a quick mode verified the whole pipeline in {{quick_minutes}} minutes, and the full analysis reproduced every value in this article. All values reported in this article are inserted programmatically from the generated result files. The code, the locked study list, the configuration files and the result files are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.
