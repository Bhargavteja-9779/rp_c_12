# 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Random splits overstate accuracy for new studies

Fig. {{FIGREF:fig01_workflow}} summarises the study design and Fig. {{FIGREF:fig02_dataset}} the data: most studies contributed between a few and a few dozen runs, a few contributed more than 100, and the source studies span four decades. Under random-row CV the gradient-boosting learners reproduced the accuracy typically reported in the literature (best bio-oil R² = {{biooil_best_random_r2}}, RMSE = {{biooil_best_random_rmse}} wt%, {{biooil_best_random_learner}}; best solid-yield RMSE = {{solid_best_random_rmse}} wt%) (Table 2, Fig. {{FIGREF:fig03_random_vs_grouped}}). When every study was predicted by a model that had not seen it, RMSE roughly doubled for the flexible learners (tree ensembles, boosting, k-NN, SVR, MLP): the ratio of study-grouped to random-row RMSE ranged from {{biooil_ratio_min}} to {{biooil_ratio_max}} for bio-oil (median {{biooil_ratio_median}}) and from {{solid_ratio_min}} to {{solid_ratio_max}} for solid yield (median {{solid_ratio_median}}). The best study-grouped RMSE was {{biooil_best_grouped_rmse}} wt% for bio-oil ({{biooil_best_grouped_learner}}, R² = {{biooil_best_grouped_r2}}) and {{solid_best_grouped_rmse}} wt% for solid yield ({{solid_best_grouped_learner}}, R² = {{solid_best_grouped_r2}}), compared with {{biooil_mean_rmse}} and {{solid_mean_rmse}} wt% for a model that always predicts the training mean. Ratios close to one were obtained only by learners that fit the data poorly in both schemes (mean predictor, ridge regression, GPBoost), i.e. the optimism of random splitting grows with the capacity of a learner to memorise study-specific patterns. Parity plots show that the near-diagonal agreement under random splitting is replaced by wide, partly systematic scatter for unseen studies (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Among learners, differences under study-grouped CV were small relative to the gap between schemes: for bio-oil, {{biooil_n_learners_not_sig_vs_best}} learners were not significantly worse than the best one ({{biooil_best_ref}}) after Holm correction on per-study RMSE (Supplementary Table S3), and the multilayer perceptron extrapolated poorly to unseen studies (RMSE {{biooil_mlp_grouped_rmse}} wt%) despite competitive random-split accuracy ({{biooil_mlp_random_rmse}} wt%). GPBoost, which models a study random intercept, gained nothing for unseen studies: because feedstock descriptors are nearly constant within a study, the random intercept absorbed most of the variance and its fixed-effect component — the only part available for a new study — was nearly flat (Supplementary Section S2). This illustrates that, in literature-compiled data, study identity and feedstock chemistry are strongly confounded, which limits how much of the between-study variation any model can attribute to chemistry.

## 3.2. Most out-of-study error is a study-level offset

For the STRATA base learner ({{strata_learner_label}}), study offsets were large and spread on both sides of zero (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig04_study_offsets}}). The between-study share of out-of-study residual variance was {{biooil_share}} (95% CI {{biooil_share_lo}}–{{biooil_share_hi}}) for bio-oil and {{solid_share}} ({{solid_share_lo}}–{{solid_share_hi}}) for solid yield; the standard deviation of study offsets ($\hat\tau$) was {{biooil_tau}} and {{solid_tau}} wt%, against within-study residual standard deviations ($\hat\sigma_w$) of {{biooil_sigw}} and {{solid_sigw}} wt% (Table 3). A model that is well calibrated on average can therefore be biased by more than 10 wt% for an individual study, and errors for runs of the same study are strongly correlated — which is why randomly held-out runs, whose study offsets were already learnt, give an optimistic picture.

This negative result has two readings, which the data cannot separate. Either work-up conventions matter less than chemistry and conditions that the curated fields do not capture (feedstock batch, particle size, reactor heat-up, quenching and product-handling details), or the coarse, partly missing descriptors that can be parsed from publications are too noisy to reveal their effect — {{none_named_pct}}% of development bio-oil runs named no extraction solvent. In both cases the practical conclusion is the same: the offset of a new study is not predictable from what studies currently report, so it has to be either measured (Section 3.7) or represented in the uncertainty (Section 3.6).

## 3.4. Laboratories agree on the direction of temperature effects, but models do not predict within-study changes better than levels

Runs that differed *only* in temperature occurred in {{biooil_nst_250_300}} development studies for bio-oil and {{solid_nst_250_300}} for solids in the 250–300 °C band alone ({{n_pairs_T_biooil}} and {{n_pairs_T_solid}} pairs over all bands). Their observed slopes trace the familiar behaviour of hydrothermal conversion [@Gollakota2018; @Funke2010] remarkably consistently across laboratories (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig11_trend_atlas}}, Supplementary Table S11): bio-oil yield changed by {{biooil_slope_150_250}} wt% per 10 °C below 250 °C (95% CI {{biooil_slope_150_250_lo}} to {{biooil_slope_150_250_hi}}; same sign in {{biooil_sign_150_250}}% of studies) and by {{biooil_slope_250_300}} wt% at 250–300 °C ({{biooil_sign_250_300}}% same sign), levelled off at 300–350 °C ({{biooil_slope_300_350}} wt%, {{biooil_slope_300_350_lo}} to {{biooil_slope_300_350_hi}}) and tended to decrease above 350 °C ({{biooil_slope_350_450}} wt%); solid yield changed by {{solid_slope_150_250}}, {{solid_slope_250_300}}, {{solid_slope_300_350}} and {{solid_slope_350_450}} wt% per 10 °C in the four bands ({{solid_sign_lt300}}% same sign below 300 °C). Doubling the residence time had no consistent effect on bio-oil yield ({{biooil_tslope}} wt%, {{biooil_tslope_lo}} to {{biooil_tslope_hi}}; {{biooil_tsign}}% same sign) and a small negative effect on solid yield ({{solid_tslope}} wt%, {{solid_tslope_lo}} to {{solid_tslope_hi}}). The direction of the temperature response is therefore shared by laboratories whose absolute yields differ by tens of wt%; its magnitude is not — the between-study standard deviation of the bio-oil slope at 250–300 °C ({{biooil_sdslope_250_300}} wt% per 10 °C) is {{biooil_sd_over_slope_250_300}} times the median slope.

On the locked studies the pattern was mixed: offset-invariant models retained contrast skill where the absolute model failed (bio-oil $R^2_\Delta$ {{biooil_l_c_r2_A}} for A vs {{biooil_l_c_r2_W}} for W and {{biooil_l_c_r2_PD}} for PD; temperature-only pairs {{biooil_l_Tonly_A}} vs {{biooil_l_Tonly_PD}}), but two studies accounted for {{lk_c_top2_share}}% of the squared contrast error of A, and per-study comparisons were not significant ({{t2_locked_sig}} of {{t2_locked_n}} after Holm; smallest unadjusted *p* = {{t2_locked_minp}}). The two analyses are reconciled by the size of the within-study variation: because laboratories agree on direction but not on magnitude, and most within-study pairs also differ in catalyst, medium or feedstock rather than temperature alone, the within-study residual variance ($\hat\sigma_w$ = {{biooil_sigw}} wt% for bio-oil) is large compared with the systematic within-study differences that the inputs explain. Directional knowledge of the kind shown in Fig. {{FIGREF:fig11_trend_atlas}} is therefore a robust prior for experimental design, but literature-trained models should not be trusted to quantify the effect of a planned change in a new laboratory.

## 3.5. Choosing the best planned run: an exploratory advantage that did not replicate

## 3.6. Row-calibrated intervals under-cover new studies; study calibration largely restores coverage

Intervals calibrated on randomly held-out runs are narrow because the residuals of seen studies omit the study offset. For unseen development studies, nominal 90% row-calibrated intervals covered {{biooil_cov_RC}} (bio-oil; 95% CI {{biooil_cov_RC_lo}}–{{biooil_cov_RC_hi}}) and {{solid_cov_RC}} (solids) of runs with equal study weights, and {{biooil_below80_RC}}% and {{solid_below80_RC}}% of studies had coverage below 0.80 (Table 4, Fig. {{FIGREF:fig05_coverage}}). Calibrating on out-of-study residuals with equal study weights gave {{biooil_cov_SC}} ({{biooil_cov_SC_lo}}–{{biooil_cov_SC_hi}}) and {{solid_cov_SC}}; the improvement was significant across studies (one-sided Wilcoxon on per-study coverage, *p* {{biooil_covtest_p}} for bio-oil and {{solid_covtest_p}} for solids). The price is width: {{biooil_width_SC}} vs {{biooil_width_RC}} wt% for bio-oil and {{solid_width_SC}} vs {{solid_width_RC}} wt% for solids. These widths are not a defect of the method but an honest statement of how little a literature-trained model knows about a study it has not seen. Whether this holds for other base learners, and what the machine-learning model adds, was tested by repeating the evaluation with seven base learners (E9; Supplementary Table S10). Study-calibrated coverage was {{biooil_e9_sc_min}}–{{biooil_e9_sc_max}} for bio-oil and {{solid_e9_sc_min}}–{{solid_e9_sc_max}} for solids with every base learner, including the training mean. ## 3.7. A few local runs reduce error and interval width

When *k* runs of the new study are available, its offset can be estimated. For the {{biooil_k_nstudies}} development studies with at least 11 bio-oil runs, the median per-study RMSE fell from {{biooil_k0_none_rmse}} wt% (*k* = 0) to {{biooil_k1_eb_rmse}}, {{biooil_k3_eb_rmse}} and {{biooil_k10_eb_rmse}} wt% with *k* = 1, 3 and 10 shrunk offsets, and the study-calibrated interval width fell from {{biooil_k0_none_width}} to {{biooil_k3_eb_width}} wt% at *k* = 3 while study-weighted coverage stayed at {{biooil_k3_eb_cov}} (Table 5, Fig. {{FIGREF:fig06_kshot}}). ## 3.10. What literature-trained models can and cannot do for a new laboratory

Table 8 condenses the evidence. Of the six model capabilities a new laboratory might expect, only two were supported on held-out studies: honest uncertainty, provided intervals are calibrated across studies, and the correction of predictions with a few local runs; the seventh entry, agreement of laboratories on the direction of temperature effects, is a model-free observation. Absolute bio-oil yields of a new study were barely predictable, study offsets were not explained by reported metadata, within-study changes were predicted no better than levels, and the exploratory advantage in choosing conditions did not replicate. The only robust *trend* knowledge was model-free and directional (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig11_trend_atlas}}). For practitioners, three recommendations follow. First, literature-trained hydrothermal surrogates should be validated with folds defined by source study (or by laboratory), and random-split metrics should not be used to claim predictive ability for new feedstocks or plants. Second, reported prediction intervals should be calibrated across studies; for the present database this means honest intervals of roughly ±{{biooil_halfwidth_SC}} wt% for bio-oil yield of a new study, which is wide enough to rule out fine-grained optimisation from literature data alone. Third, a handful of runs on the target feedstock and reactor are worth more than additional model complexity: three local runs reduced the median per-study error by {{biooil_k3_gain}} wt% (bio-oil) and {{solid_k3_gain}} wt% (solids), whereas the median per-study errors of the competitive learners differed by at most {{biooil_learner_span}} and {{solid_learner_span}} wt% (Sections 3.1 and 3.7). For data curators, the null result for work-up descriptors suggests recording richer, standardised metadata (product-recovery procedure, quench, mass-balance closure, uncertainty of the yield measurement) so that the sources of between-study variation can eventually be modelled rather than absorbed.

## 3.11. Limitations

The study relies on one curated literature database; its extraction and harmonisation choices, imputed feedstock fractions and residual transcription errors propagate into all results, although they affect random and study-grouped evaluation alike. The protocol descriptors were parsed from free-text fields and are coarse. Study-level conformal validity assumes that a new study is exchangeable with the calibration studies; a study that differs systematically (a new reactor concept, unusual feedstock or catalyst) may be under-covered, as the locked solid-yield studies (coverage {{solid_lock_cov_SC}}) and the HTC subset of the development studies (coverage {{htc_cov_SC}} for solids) show. The external database lacks study identifiers, so feedstock series were used as pseudo-studies. The *k*-shot analysis assumes that the first *k* runs of a study are exchangeable with its remaining runs; in practice laboratories choose runs deliberately. Physical admissibility across phases was not enforced here; because STRATA only requires out-of-study residuals of a point predictor (Supplementary Table S10), it could be applied to physically admissible surrogates, but this was not tested. Cross-study duplicate runs (Section 3.8) were retained in the main analyses, which makes the reported study-grouped accuracy slightly optimistic. Three analyses (E9–E11), the adaptive-interval variant and the locked-test sensitivity analysis were added after the main results were known. Finally, no new experiments were performed: the conclusions concern the transferability of literature-trained models, not the chemistry of hydrothermal conversion itself.
