## 3.1. Random splits overstate accuracy for new studies

Fig. {{FIGREF:fig01_workflow}} summarises the study design. Under random-row CV the gradient-boosting learners reproduced the accuracy typically reported in the literature (best bio-oil R² = {{biooil_best_random_r2}}, RMSE = {{biooil_best_random_rmse}} wt%, {{biooil_best_random_learner}}; best solid-yield RMSE = {{solid_best_random_rmse}} wt%) ({{TREF:table2_benchmark}}, Fig. {{FIGREF:fig03_random_vs_grouped}}). When every study was predicted by a model that had not seen it, RMSE roughly doubled for the flexible learners: the ratio of study-grouped to random-row RMSE ranged from {{biooil_ratio_min}} to {{biooil_ratio_max}} for bio-oil (median {{biooil_ratio_median}}) and from {{solid_ratio_min}} to {{solid_ratio_max}} for solid yield (median {{solid_ratio_median}}). The best study-grouped RMSE was {{biooil_best_grouped_rmse}} wt% for bio-oil ({{biooil_best_grouped_learner}}, R² = {{biooil_best_grouped_r2}}) and {{solid_best_grouped_rmse}} wt% for solid yield ({{solid_best_grouped_learner}}, R² = {{solid_best_grouped_r2}}), compared with {{biooil_mean_rmse}} and {{solid_mean_rmse}} wt% for a model that always predicts the training mean. Ratios close to one were obtained only by learners that fit the data poorly in both schemes (mean predictor, ridge regression, GPBoost); the optimism of random splitting grows with the capacity of a learner to memorise study-specific patterns. Differences between learners under study-grouped CV were small relative to the gap between schemes ({{biooil_n_learners_not_sig_vs_best}} learners not significantly worse than the best for bio-oil; {{TREF:tableS_paired_learner_tests}}).

## 3.2. Most out-of-study error is a study offset that reported metadata do not explain

For the STRATA base learner ({{strata_learner_label}}), study offsets were large and spread on both sides of zero (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig04_study_offsets}}). The between-study share of out-of-study residual variance was {{biooil_share}} (95% CI {{biooil_share_lo}}–{{biooil_share_hi}}) for bio-oil and {{solid_share}} ({{solid_share_lo}}–{{solid_share_hi}}) for solid yield; the standard deviation of study offsets ($\hat\tau$) was {{biooil_tau}} and {{solid_tau}} wt%, against within-study residual standard deviations ($\hat\sigma_w$) of {{biooil_sigw}} and {{solid_sigw}} wt% ({{TREF:table3_heterogeneity}}). A model that is well calibrated on average can therefore be biased by more than 10 wt% for an individual laboratory, and errors for runs of the same study are strongly correlated.

{{P_sentence}} Adding the protocol descriptors changed the median per-study RMSE from {{biooil_P_med_base}} to {{biooil_P_med_baseP}} wt% for bio-oil (one-sided Wilcoxon *p* {{biooil_P_p}}; *n* = {{biooil_P_n}} studies) and from {{solid_P_med_base}} to {{solid_P_med_baseP}} wt% for solid yield (*p* {{solid_P_p}}). A ridge meta-regression of the study offsets on study-level descriptors had no out-of-study predictive value (leave-one-study-out R² = {{biooil_mr_r2}} and {{solid_mr_r2}}), and offsets did not differ significantly between the dominant extraction solvents ({{kw_sentence}}). Either work-up conventions matter less than unrecorded details (feedstock batch, particle size, heat-up, quenching, product handling), or the coarse descriptors that can be parsed from publications — {{none_named_pct}}% of development bio-oil runs named no extraction solvent — are too noisy to reveal their effect. In both cases the offset of a new laboratory is not predictable from what studies currently report: it must be represented in the uncertainty (Section 3.3) or measured (Section 3.4).

## 3.3. Study-calibrated intervals keep their coverage for new studies, at the price of width

For unseen development studies, nominal 90% row-calibrated intervals covered {{biooil_cov_RC}} (bio-oil; 95% CI {{biooil_cov_RC_lo}}–{{biooil_cov_RC_hi}}) and {{solid_cov_RC}} (solids) of runs with equal study weights, and {{biooil_below80_RC}}% and {{solid_below80_RC}}% of studies had coverage below 0.80 ({{TREF:table4_coverage}}, Fig. {{FIGREF:fig05_coverage}}). Interval width is the central quantity for a laboratory, because it states how much the prediction can be trusted before any experiment. Study-calibrated intervals were {{biooil_width_SC}} wt% wide for bio-oil and {{solid_width_SC}} wt% for solids, against {{biooil_width_RC}} and {{solid_width_RC}} wt% for row-calibrated ones — an honest half-width of roughly ±{{biooil_halfwidth_SC}} wt% for the bio-oil yield of a new study, which rules out fine-grained optimisation from literature data alone. ## 3.5. Decision support: can a laboratory tell in advance, and how many runs does it need?

The applicability-domain score ranked the risk of a large error: for development studies its Spearman correlation with the error of the unadapted model was {{ad_biooil_rho}} for bio-oil (*p* = {{ad_biooil_p}}; {{ad_biooil_n}} studies) and {{ad_solid_rho}} for solids (*p* = {{ad_solid_p}}) ({{TREF:table_v3_decision}}). The pre-registered threshold, however, flagged {{ad_biooil_lflag}} of {{ad_biooil_lnst}} locked bio-oil studies and {{ad_solid_lflag}} of {{ad_solid_lnst}} locked solid studies (largest scores {{ad_biooil_lmax}} and {{ad_solid_lmax}} against thresholds {{ad_biooil_thr}} and {{ad_solid_thr}}), so hypothesis D1 could not be tested and the rule "use local runs if flagged" reduced to always using STRATA. In an exploratory analysis added after this result, the score still correlated with the error on locked bio-oil studies (ρ = {{ad_biooil_lrho}}, *p* = {{ad_biooil_lp}}; {{ad_biooil_leval}} studies) but not on locked solid studies (ρ = {{ad_solid_lrho}}, *p* = {{ad_solid_lp}}). The score did not identify the locked bio-oil studies for which local runs alone were better (Section 3.4): being close to the training data in feedstock and conditions does not guarantee that a laboratory's yields behave like the literature average. A laboratory should therefore not rely on a distance-based flag alone but compare literature-anchored and local-only predictions once its first runs are available, as STRATA-Lab prescribes (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig16_strata_lab_workflow}}).

How many runs are needed depends on the precision required. With STRATA, the development half-width of the 90% interval was ±{{hw_biooil_0}}, ±{{hw_biooil_1}}, ±{{hw_biooil_2}}, ±{{hw_biooil_3}} and ±{{hw_biooil_5}} wt% for bio-oil after 0, 1, 2, 3 and 5 runs, and ±{{hw_solid_0}}, ±{{hw_solid_1}}, ±{{hw_solid_2}}, ±{{hw_solid_3}} and ±{{hw_solid_5}} wt% for solids. A half-width of ±20 wt% was reached for solids after {{need_solid_20}} run (locked coverage {{need_solid_20_lcov}}, median RMSE {{need_solid_20_lrmse}} wt%) but for bio-oil not within five runs, and ±10 or ±15 wt% was not reached for either target. Literature-trained models anchored by a few runs therefore support screening — deciding whether a feedstock is promising and which temperature region to explore — but not the optimisation of conditions to within a few wt%, which requires a local experimental design.

## 3.8. What did not transfer: within-study trends and the choice of conditions

If most of the error for a new laboratory is an offset, *changes* between runs of the same study might transfer even when absolute yields do not. Model-free, laboratories agreed on the direction of the temperature response below 300 °C: bio-oil yield rose by {{biooil_slope_250_300}} wt% per 10 °C at 250–300 °C (same sign in {{biooil_sign_250_300}}% of studies) and solid yield fell ({{solid_sign_lt300}}% same sign) ({{FREF:fig11_trend_atlas}}, {{TREF:table_v2_empirical_trends}}), but the between-study standard deviation of the bio-oil slope was {{biooil_sd_over_slope_250_300}} times its median. Contrary to the pre-registered hypotheses, learners did not predict within-study changes better than absolute yields: contrast $R^2_\Delta$ for unseen development studies was {{biooil_d_c_r2_min}}–{{biooil_d_c_r2_max}} for bio-oil and {{solid_d_c_r2_min}}–{{solid_d_c_r2_max}} for solids, offset-invariant learners were better than the absolute model in {{t2_dev_sig}} of {{t2_dev_n}} Holm-corrected comparisons, and anchoring with offset-invariant models did not improve on the STRATA offset ({{FREF:fig12_levels_vs_contrasts}}, {{TREF:table_v2_levels_vs_contrasts}}). An exploratory advantage of the models over a 325 °C rule of thumb in choosing the best of a study's planned bio-oil runs {{c1_sentence}} on the locked studies (median regret {{biooil_rk_l_A}}–{{biooil_rk_l_W}} wt% vs {{biooil_rk_l_rule}} wt% for the rule; one-sided Holm-corrected *p* {{biooil_rk_l_p_PD}}; {{FREF:fig13_ranking_regret}}). Directional knowledge is thus a robust prior for planning, but literature-trained models should not be trusted to quantify the effect of a planned change or to select optimal conditions in a new laboratory.

## 3.9. STRATA-Lab in practice

{{TREF:table_v3_capability_matrix}} condenses the evidence. Of the capabilities a new laboratory might expect from a literature-trained hydrothermal model, three were supported on held-out studies: honest uncertainty, provided intervals are calibrated across studies; the correction of predictions with a few local runs; and, as a model-free observation, the direction of the temperature effect. Absolute bio-oil yields of a new study were barely predictable, offsets were not explained by reported metadata, within-study changes and rankings did not transfer, the applicability-domain threshold flagged no failing study, and for one held-out set the local runs alone outperformed literature anchoring.

{{TABLE:table_v3_capability_matrix}}

A laboratory starting with a new feedstock can therefore proceed as follows (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig16_strata_lab_workflow}}). (1) Obtain the literature prediction with a study-calibrated interval (about ±{{hw_biooil_0}} wt% for bio-oil and ±{{hw_solid_0}} wt% for solid yield) and read it as a screening statement, not as an optimum. (2) Compute the applicability-domain score; a high score is a warning, but a low score is no guarantee. (3) Measure one to three runs in a screening design — centre, lowest and highest temperature of the intended window. (4) Compare the STRATA prediction with the local mean of these runs; where they diverge, as for the locked bio-oil studies and the microalga case, weight the local evidence and extend the local design rather than the model. (5) Report predictions with the re-calibrated intervals and the number of runs on which they rest. {{TREF:table_v3_checklist}} lists the corresponding minimum validation and reporting practice for authors and reviewers of literature-trained bioresource models. Monetary savings were not estimated, because experimental costs vary too widely between laboratories for a general figure to be meaningful.

## 3.10. Limitations

All validation is retrospective: published runs of held-out studies played the role of a new laboratory's experiments, and no new experiments were performed. The reported order of runs in the database follows the source tables and is not the chronological order of the experiments; the screening-design order is an idealisation of how a laboratory would choose its first runs, and a laboratory that chooses runs differently may see different gains. The study relies on one curated literature database, whose extraction and harmonisation choices, imputed feedstock fractions and residual transcription errors (for example, the 20 °C entry of the solid-yield case study) propagate into all results. Only bio-oil and solid yields were modelled; aqueous and gas phases, product quality and mass closure were not. Study-level conformal validity assumes that a new study is exchangeable with the calibration studies; studies that differ systematically were under-covered, as the locked solid-yield studies show. The external database lacks study identifiers, so feedstock series were used as pseudo-studies. The applicability-domain threshold flagged no locked study, so its value as a decision rule could not be tested. With 18–19 eligible locked studies per target, the held-out deployment comparisons have limited power. The protocol was registered by timestamped repository commits rather than in a public registry, and several analyses (E9–E11, the adaptive-interval variant and the exploratory locked correlation of the applicability-domain score) were added after results were known.
