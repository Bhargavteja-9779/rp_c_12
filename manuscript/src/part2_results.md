# 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1. Evidence base

Of the 150 development studies, {{ma_n_studies}} contained at least one within-study contrast, giving {{ma_n_contrasts}} contrasts across seven outcomes ({{ma_biooil_contrasts}} for bio-oil yield from {{ma_biooil_studies}} studies and {{ma_solid_contrasts}} for solid yield from {{ma_solid_studies}} studies). Temperature (up to {{m_solid_T_250_300_k}} studies per band), residence time and catalysts were studied most often; solid loading and reaction media much less (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig18_evidence_map}}). Of the {{ma_n_items}} outcome × factor × level combinations with at least five studies, {{ma_n_items_sig}} had a 95% confidence interval that excluded zero. The pooled means were robust to the choice of the $\tau^2$ estimator (largest difference between REML and Paule–Mandel {{ma_pm_maxdiff}} units), to using one contrast per study and to leaving out single studies, and to resampling whole studies (a study-level bootstrap removed significance from {{boot_lost}} of the {{ma_n_items_sig}} effects whose CI excluded zero, and was less conservative than the Hartung–Knapp intervals for {{boot_gained}} combinations with few studies, for which the more conservative intervals are reported), and {{ma_egger_sig}} of {{ma_egger_n}} Egger-type tests indicated small-study effects at *p* < 0.05, close to the number expected by chance ({{TREF:table_v4_sensitivity}}).

## 3.2. Temperature and residence time

Within studies, bio-oil yield rose with temperature up to 300 °C — by {{m_biooil_T_lt250}} wt% per 10 °C below 250 °C (95% CI {{m_biooil_T_lt250_lo}} to {{m_biooil_T_lt250_hi}}; {{m_biooil_T_lt250_k}} studies) and {{m_biooil_T_250_300}} wt% at 250–300 °C ({{m_biooil_T_250_300_lo}} to {{m_biooil_T_250_300_hi}}; {{m_biooil_T_250_300_k}} studies) — and then declined slightly, by {{m_biooil_T_300_350_abs}} wt% per 10 °C at 300–350 °C (95% CI {{m_biooil_T_300_350_lo}} to {{m_biooil_T_300_350_hi}}) and {{m_biooil_T_ge350_abs}} wt% above 350 °C (CI including zero) (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig19_effect_atlas}}, {{TREF:table_v4_meta_primary}}). Solid yield fell most steeply at low temperature (by {{m_solid_T_lt250_abs}} and {{m_solid_T_250_300_abs}} wt% per 10 °C below 250 °C and at 250–300 °C) and only slightly above 300 °C (by {{m_solid_T_300_350_abs}} wt%). These within-study estimates quantify the temperature optimum for bio-oil that reviews have discussed qualitatively [@Akhtar2011; @Gollakota2018]: the gain from 250 to 300 °C is about {{lab_biooil_50C}} wt%, and on average nothing is gained above 300 °C. At the same time, gas yield increased with temperature, most clearly above 300 °C (by {{m_gas_T_300_350}} wt% per 10 °C at 300–350 °C and {{m_gas_T_ge350}} wt% above 350 °C), and the HHV of bio-oil rose by {{m_hhv_biooil_T_250_300}} MJ kg^−1^ per 10 °C at 250–300 °C with no detectable heterogeneity (I² = {{m_hhv_biooil_T_250_300_I2}}) ({{TREF:table_v4_meta_secondary}}). Energy recovery to bio-oil therefore increased by {{m_energy_biooil_T_250_300}} percentage points per 10 °C at 250–300 °C but not above 300 °C. The bio-oil temperature effect at 250–300 °C was comparatively consistent between laboratories (I² = {{m_biooil_T_250_300_I2}}; P(increase) = {{m_biooil_T_250_300_pb}}), whereas above 300 °C laboratories disagreed on the sign (I² = {{m_biooil_T_300_350_I2}}).

Doubling the residence time did not change bio-oil yield on average ({{m_biooil_t_all}} wt%, {{m_biooil_t_all_lo}} to {{m_biooil_t_all_hi}}; {{m_biooil_t_all_k}} studies) and tended to lower solid yield ({{m_solid_t_all}} wt%, {{m_solid_t_all_lo}} to {{m_solid_t_all_hi}}), while it raised bio-oil HHV by {{m_hhv_biooil_t_all}} MJ kg^−1^. For most HTL studies, holding time is therefore a lever for oil quality rather than for oil yield.

## 3.3. Catalysts and reaction media redistribute products

All three well-studied catalyst classes increased bio-oil yield within studies: alkali carbonates by {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate}} wt% ({{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_lo}} to {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_hi}}; {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_k}} studies), alkali hydroxides by {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide}} wt% ({{m_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide_lo}} to {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide_hi}}; {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide_k}} studies) and metal or supported-metal catalysts by {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported}} wt% ({{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_lo}} to {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_hi}}; {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_k}} studies). On a relative scale these are increases of {{rr_biooil_alkali_carbonate}}%, {{rr_biooil_alkali_hydroxide}}% and {{rr_biooil_metal_supported}}% over the uncatalysed yield ({{TREF:table_v4_relative}}). The same catalysts reduced solid yield by {{m_solid_cat_alkali_carbonate_abs}}, {{m_solid_cat_alkali_hydroxide_abs}} and {{m_solid_cat_metal_supported_abs}} wt%, and alkali hydroxides increased the aqueous-phase yield by {{m_aqueous_cat_alkali_hydroxide}} wt% ({{m_aqueous_cat_alkali_hydroxide_lo}} to {{m_aqueous_cat_alkali_hydroxide_hi}}), whereas gas yields did not change significantly (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig20_phase_redistribution}}). Catalysts thus act mainly by shifting mass from the solid to the liquid products rather than by additional gasification, consistent with the commonly proposed suppression of char-forming condensation reactions [@Akhtar2011; @Peterson2008]; single-study investigations of alkali catalysts [@Zhu2015] can now be placed against a cross-study average. Because alkali carbonates and metal catalysts also raised bio-oil HHV (by {{m_hhv_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate}} and {{m_hhv_biooil_cat_metal_supported}} MJ kg^−1^, respectively), the energy recovered in bio-oil increased by {{m_energy_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate}} (alkali carbonate), {{m_energy_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide}} (alkali hydroxide) and {{m_energy_biooil_cat_metal_supported}} percentage points (metal/supported) — the largest process effects found in this study (relative increases of {{rr_energy_biooil_alkali_hydroxide}}–{{rr_energy_biooil_metal_supported}}%). Bio-oil nitrogen content changed by at most {{n_biooil_maxabs}} wt% with any catalyst class or 10 °C temperature step, and no such change was significant ({{TREF:table_v4_meta_nitrogen}}), so catalysts in these studies did not measurably reduce the nitrogen burden of the oil.

In an exploratory dose–response analysis registered before it was computed (Addendum F; {{TREF:table_v4_dose}}), doubling the loading of a metal or supported-metal catalyst raised bio-oil yield by a further {{dose_biooil_metal_supported}} wt% ({{dose_biooil_metal_supported_lo}} to {{dose_biooil_metal_supported_hi}}; {{dose_biooil_metal_supported_k}} studies), whereas doubling the loading of an alkali carbonate changed it by only {{dose_biooil_alkali_carbonate}} wt% ({{dose_biooil_alkali_carbonate_lo}} to {{dose_biooil_alkali_carbonate_hi}}; {{dose_biooil_alkali_carbonate_k}} studies) — most of the benefit of alkali appears to come from its presence rather than its amount within the loadings studied, although few studies varied the dose.

The pre-specified moderator analyses found no catalyst effect that depended on temperature, process subtype or feedstock lignin content after Holm correction for the primary outcomes ({{mod_nsig_holm}} of {{mod_n}} tests significant after correction, for energy recovery with alkali hydroxides: a larger gain for low-lignin feedstocks; {{TREF:table_v4_moderators}}). Co-solvents were studied in too few laboratories for firm conclusions: water–alcohol mixtures lowered solid yield by {{m_solid_med_water_alcohol_abs}} wt% ({{m_solid_med_water_alcohol_k}} studies), and the confidence intervals of all other medium effects included zero.

## 3.4. Feedstock composition and solid loading

Studies that processed several feedstocks under identical conditions allow composition effects to be estimated free of laboratory offsets ({{TREF:table_v4_feedstock}}). For bio-oil yield ({{fs_biooil_studies}} studies, {{fs_biooil_cells}} cells), none of the five composition variables had a significant effect once the others were held constant (lignin {{fs_biooil_lignin}} wt% per +10 wt%, 95% CI {{fs_biooil_lignin_lo}} to {{fs_biooil_lignin_hi}}). Solid yield decreased with hemicellulose content ({{fs_solid_hemicellulose}} wt% per +10 wt%, {{fs_solid_hemicellulose_lo}} to {{fs_solid_hemicellulose_hi}}; {{fs_solid_studies}} studies), and bio-oil nitrogen increased with feedstock nitrogen ({{fs_n_biooil_N}} wt% per +10 wt% feed N, i.e. about {{fs_n_biooil_N_per1}} wt% of oil N per wt% of feed N) and ash. Temperature and catalyst effects were broadly similar for agricultural residues, woody biomass and technical lignins (exploratory, Addendum F; {{TREF:table_v4_family}}); the only difference that survived Holm correction ({{fam_nsig_holm}} of {{fam_n}} tests) was a larger reduction of solid yield by metal catalysts for woody biomass ({{fam_solid_cat_metal_supported_wood}} wt%, {{fam_solid_cat_metal_supported_wood_k}} studies) than for agricultural residues ({{fam_solid_cat_metal_supported_agri}} wt%, {{fam_solid_cat_metal_supported_agri_k}} studies). Higher solid loading increased solid yield by {{m_solid_load_all}} wt% per +10 percentage points ({{m_solid_load_all_lo}} to {{m_solid_load_all_hi}}; {{m_solid_load_all_k}} studies), whereas its effect on bio-oil yield was inconsistent ({{m_biooil_load_all}} wt%, CI {{m_biooil_load_all_lo}} to {{m_biooil_load_all_hi}}). The within-study evidence on composition comes from fewer studies and a narrower composition range than the pooled data, so the absence of a significant composition effect on bio-oil yield does not rule out a moderate one.

## 3.5. Effects vary between laboratories, and the laboratory itself matters more

Heterogeneity was the rule rather than the exception: the median I² was {{ma_I2_median}}, and for catalysts the 95% prediction intervals for a new study spanned zero (bio-oil, alkali carbonate: {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_plo}} to {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_phi}} wt%; metal/supported: {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_plo}} to {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_phi}} wt%). Expressed as probabilities, adding an alkali carbonate, an alkali hydroxide or a metal catalyst is expected to increase bio-oil yield in {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_pbpct}}, {{m_biooil_cat_alkali_hydroxide_pbpct}} and {{m_biooil_cat_metal_supported_pbpct}} of new laboratories, and raising the temperature from 250 towards 300 °C in {{m_biooil_T_250_300_pbpct}}. In other words, the direction of the main process effects is reliable on average, but a laboratory has a 15–30% chance that a change which helps elsewhere will not help in its own system.

The laboratory effect itself was larger than any process effect on yields (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig21_lab_vs_process}}). After accounting for feedstock composition and all recorded conditions, the standard deviation of study-level offsets was {{biooil_tau}} wt% for bio-oil yield when estimated from out-of-study residuals of a gradient-boosting model, and {{lab_mm_biooil_tau}} wt% in a linear mixed model (intraclass correlation {{lab_mm_biooil_icc}}); for solid yield it was {{solid_tau}} and {{lab_mm_solid_tau}} wt% (intraclass correlation {{lab_mm_solid_icc}}). A one-standard-deviation difference between laboratories is thus {{lab_biooil_ratio_cat_min}}–{{lab_biooil_ratio_cat_max}} times the effect of adding a catalyst on bio-oil yield and corresponds to a temperature change of about {{lab_biooil_equivT_ml}}–{{lab_biooil_equivT_mm}} °C at the 250–300 °C slope — more than the whole range over which temperature raises yields. The laboratory effect comprises everything that is constant within a study and not captured by the recorded inputs: unrecorded feedstock properties, reactor heat-up and quenching, product recovery and the definition of yields. Inter-laboratory differences of this kind are known for the analysis of liquefaction oils [@IEARR2020]; the present results show that they also dominate yields, and explain why literature-trained models predict new studies poorly (Supplementary Section S6).

## 3.6. What pooled machine-learning models get right and wrong

Pooled ML models are widely used to infer process effects from literature data. Here, the partial-dependence effects of a pooled XGBoost model agreed well with the within-study meta-analysis (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig22_pooled_vs_meta}}, {{TREF:table_v4_pooled}}): Spearman ρ = {{px_rho}} across {{px_n}} effects, no sign reversal among the {{px_nsig}} effects whose CI excluded zero, and {{px_out}} of model-implied effects outside the meta-analytic CI. A pooled linear model performed worse (ρ = {{pl_rho}}; {{pl_out}} outside the CI; {{pl_rev}} sign reversal, for {{pl_rev_item}}: {{pl_rev_pooled}} vs {{pl_rev_meta}} wt%), whereas adding study fixed effects to the linear model restored agreement (ρ = {{pf_rho}}; {{pf_out}} outside the CI). Flexible pooled models therefore recovered average process effects reasonably well — presumably because, with many studies, laboratory offsets act largely as noise that averages out. What they cannot provide is the between-laboratory spread, which a single partial-dependence curve or SHAP plot hides. Their importance measures should also be read with care: TreeSHAP ranked feedstock composition as the most important factor group for both yields (mean |SHAP| {{shap_biooil_feed}} wt% for bio-oil, against {{shap_biooil_cat}} wt% for catalysts and {{shap_biooil_T}} wt% for temperature, which ranked {{shap_biooil_T_rank}}th), whereas within-study comparisons of feedstocks under identical conditions found no significant composition effect on bio-oil yield (Section 3.4). Because feedstock and laboratory are strongly confounded in literature data — most studies use one or two feedstocks — part of the importance attributed to feedstock composition is likely to reflect laboratory differences.

## 3.7. Replication in held-out studies

In the 38 locked studies, {{lk_n_contrasts}} within-study contrasts from {{lk_n_studies}} studies were available. Temperature effects replicated in direction and approximately in size: the median locked-study effect of +10 °C at 250–300 °C was {{lk_biooil_T_250_300_med}} wt% for bio-oil ({{lk_biooil_T_250_300_k}} studies; development mean {{m_biooil_T_250_300}}) and {{lk_solid_T_250_300_med}} wt% for solids ({{lk_solid_T_250_300_k}} studies; development {{m_solid_T_250_300}}) (Fig. {{FIGREF:fig23_locked_replication}}). Catalyst effects in the few locked studies that tested them were scattered within the wide prediction intervals, with a median of {{lk_biooil_cat_metal_supported_med}} wt% for metal catalysts on bio-oil ({{lk_biooil_cat_metal_supported_k}} studies) but {{lk_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_med}} wt% for alkali carbonates ({{lk_biooil_cat_alkali_carbonate_k}} studies). Overall, {{lk_picov_pct}} of the {{lk_n_eff}} locked-study effects fell within the 95% prediction intervals — fewer than nominal, so the intervals are, if anything, too narrow. The pre-registered confirmatory test was not supported: predicting each locked study's contrasts with the meta-analytic mean gave a median per-study RMSE of {{lk_rmse_meta}} wt%, against {{lk_rmse_xgb}} wt% for the pooled XGBoost model and {{lk_rmse_zero}} wt% for assuming no change (Holm-corrected *p* = {{lk_p_xgb}} and {{lk_p_zero}}; {{TREF:table_v4_locked}}). Average effects, however well estimated, do not predict what a particular laboratory will observe, because between-laboratory variation and run-to-run scatter are as large as the effects themselves.

## 3.8. Implications for experimental planning, data-driven models and reporting

For laboratories planning HTL or HTC experiments, the effect sizes in {{TREF:table_v4_meta_primary}} and {{TREF:table_v4_meta_secondary}} provide quantitative priors: operate HTL near 300 °C rather than at 250 °C (about +{{lab_biooil_50C}} wt% bio-oil from 250 to 300 °C, no average gain above 300 °C, higher HHV), use residence time to adjust oil quality rather than yield, and expect a catalyst to raise bio-oil yield by about 5–9 wt% and energy recovery by 12–20 percentage points. The prediction intervals and probabilities of increase state how much confidence these priors deserve in a new system, and the laboratory effect implies that absolute yields reported elsewhere cannot be expected in one's own reactor. A few local runs at the intended conditions are therefore indispensable; in our earlier evaluation of literature-trained models, three locally measured runs reduced the median error for a new study by {{gain3_min}}–{{gain3_max}} wt% (Supplementary Section S6).

For data-driven modelling, the results support three practices: validate models on held-out studies rather than random runs; report effects with between-study prediction intervals rather than single partial-dependence curves; and do not interpret SHAP importance of feedstock descriptors as a causal effect without within-study evidence. For experimental reporting, the dominance of laboratory effects argues for recording the product-recovery solvent and yield basis, the heating and quenching profile, mass-balance closure and replicate variability — information that would allow future meta-analyses to explain, rather than only measure, between-laboratory variation.

## 3.9. Limitations

The analysis rests on one curated database; its harmonisation choices and any transcription errors propagate into all estimates, and its studies were selected by the curators rather than by a systematic search for all HTL/HTC experiments, so the synthesis is representative of lignocellulosic feedstocks but not of algae, sludges or manures. Small-study tests did not indicate reporting bias, but a tendency to publish catalysts that work could inflate catalyst effects. Within-study contrasts remove laboratory offsets but not confounding by factors that change together within a study; cells were defined by all recorded conditions, but unrecorded differences between runs (for example, a changed heating rate) cannot be excluded. Sampling variances were derived from the scatter of contrasts within studies because replicate standard deviations are rarely reported, and contrasts of the same study share runs; the one-contrast-per-study sensitivity analysis gave similar means, but the confidence intervals may be somewhat narrow. Catalyst classes are broad and catalyst loading was not modelled as a dose; co-solvent, acid-catalyst and loading effects rest on few studies. The laboratory effect is estimated relative to the recorded inputs and includes unrecorded feedstock and process differences. The protocol was registered by timestamped repository commits rather than in a public registry, two implementation deviations were made after the first development run (Section 2.7), and the locked studies had been used in earlier, unrelated analyses of model transfer. No new experiments were performed.
